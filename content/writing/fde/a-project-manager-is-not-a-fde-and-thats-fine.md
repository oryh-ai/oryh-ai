# A Project Manager Is Not an FDE. That Is Fine.

*When Agents Run the Business · A development note*

The best project managers I know can bring several factions inside a customer company to the same table, make sure questions get answered and plans get owners, and keep risks from exploding the day before launch. That is difficult and valuable work. When all such on-site leads are renamed FDEs, I think it diminishes project management.

The person is doing a job worth respecting, yet the pitch implies that only an “AI expert” badge makes it prestigious. The customer can no longer tell what they have bought.

## Two people can launch a project in different ways

Imagine two on-site leads. Person A gathers requirements, schedules interviews, tracks interface work, convenes meetings when departments disagree, and protects the launch window. Person B does those things too, but can also identify an approval design likely to cause duplicate payment, notice an Agent reading an expired policy, specify a missing record-layer constraint, and take a general gap back to product.

A is an excellent project manager. B may be what we mean by FDE. Both contribute to delivery. The difference is not “who writes code?” It is who owns judgments where technology and business meet. Calling A an FDE does not give them B's capability. Making B only chase schedule wastes theirs.

Some projects need a very strong project manager without a senior technical specialist living on site. Others have high business risk and a product boundary still being explored, and need someone like B. Customers should choose for the work, not for a fashionable acronym.

Their results should not be measured as though they were the same job. A kept the launch on schedule and solved cross-department coordination; B caught a dangerous automated action before it could become a repeatable mistake. The first is not “one level less capable,” and the second is not necessarily better at managing a schedule. Honest roles help the team put the right problem with the right person.

## Mixed roles leave problems without an owner

A customer says, “Urgent orders may ship early.” A project manager can convene sales, warehouse, and finance to set the scope and turn the answer into a tracked action. That is their work. If the meeting does not establish who may mark an order urgent, they keep the issue open for an authorized person to resolve.

An FDE should also spot a deeper set of risks: are stock reservations distinct from physical shipments? Can the shipment API be invoked twice? Will an Agent treat “go ahead” in a group chat as formal approval? The project manager need not personally solve these technical questions, but someone on the team must own them.

When everybody is called FDE, the customer assumes the first person on site can solve everything. Inside the supplier, the engineers may assume “the FDE is already there” and wait for a ticket. A gap that belongs in the product gets bounced across the role boundary. The grander the title, the easier it becomes for responsibility to blur.

The same goes for on-site developers. An engineer who connects a bank interface, handles retries, and corrects field mapping is doing work worth respecting. They can deliver alongside an FDE. Their integration should not be sold as “enterprise AI strategic deployment” just because the title is in fashion. The customer bought a reliable interface, not English initials.

## Give the buyer an honest staffing plan

A project plan can state the roles plainly. Who owns the schedule and cross-department coordination? Who can make technical product decisions? Who has industry experience to review business rules? Who builds customer-specific integrations? Who takes unusual cases after launch? One person may cover several roles, but state their skills and available time. Do not hide a missing role under “we have an FDE.”

On site, each unresolved issue can have an owner rather than “FDE to follow up.” “Customer to define urgency” belongs with an authorized business lead. “Concurrent stock reservation check” belongs with product engineering. “Cross-department meeting next week” belongs with the project manager. All three matter; a grand title on one task card solves none of them. The project record should show who can actually decide.

Acceptance criteria can be separate too. The project manager's work is visible in clear milestones, early risk escalation, and a sound handoff. The developer's work is a stable interface, correct data, and recoverable failures. A genuine FDE should also show that Agent judgments have evidence, hard boundaries hold, and someone can handle significant exceptions. Nobody needs to fight for the most expensive-sounding name to make their contribution real.

This helps purchasing decisions. A customer with a limited budget may choose a mature product plus a dependable project manager, bringing in a senior specialist for high-risk reviews as needed. They need not buy a “universal FDE” on site indefinitely. If the project really is complex, they can see why the cost is high and who owns which judgments.

The supplier avoids misassignment too. Label a skilled coordinator as someone who can independently make architecture and business judgments, and a technical dispute leaves them pretending certainty or calling headquarters constantly. Assign a deep technical specialist to chase meetings all day, and their skill is wasted. Honest naming is not a matter of politeness; it reduces delivery risk and wasted talent.

## Why the label irritates me

AI should help us distinguish more clearly what software already does and what a person on site still has to decide. If software handles records and an Agent handles business logic, field staff help establish rules, evidence, permissions, and product boundaries. Some coordinate, some build, some make difficult judgments. That division can deliver very well.

Call everyone FDE and we are back to a story about one person doing it all. If something fails, say the FDE was not trained enough. If the project is expensive, say the customer would not pay for expertise. If on-site code remains necessary, say “forward deployment includes development.” A handsome title saves the supplier from explaining its product boundary and staffing choices.

I do not object to a team with real FDEs using the title. I object to selling a project manager under a new name as an AI expert, and to making a project manager feel their actual profession is somehow lesser. Many projects can be delivered by good project management and good software. When senior field judgment is needed, hire and name that contribution honestly.

In Oryh, we want software to keep dependable records of documents, approvals, and actions, while Agents advance the work under company rules. A project manager can help customers articulate those rules and give the launch an owner. A developer can build the interface and hard constraints. A real FDE should identify high-risk judgments between the two and bring recurring problems back into the product. All three contributions are real. None needs to masquerade as another.
