# What Pays for a Real FDE on a Low-Bid Project?

*When Agents Run the Business · A development note*

A bid promises a “senior FDE,” but the contract price has been pushed down to the cheapest person-days. After the win, one person on site is expected to be business adviser, architect, developer, trainer, and project manager. When a scope question appears, they manage the customer's frustration while asking the team back home what to do.

This is not an accusation about a particular project. It is a budget contradiction that is easy to create. If a real FDE can independently judge industry, organization, product, and engineering risks, the supplier has to fund their time and support. A job title cannot produce such a person when the price does not cover them.

## The bid decides what the person can be

Under pressure to bid low, the first work cut is often work that is not immediately visible: field discovery before launch, testing with awkward historical cases, checking authority across departments, and reviewing failures afterward. A proposal can describe a complete delivery method while the budget only funds data entry, interface setup, and a workflow good enough for a demo.

Senior capacity also costs money when it is not fully utilized. A project may not face a critical issue every day, but the right person must be available when one appears. If the supplier can fund people only through billable days, it will spread the strongest person across several customers or put them at the kickoff before handing the site to a cheaper colleague. On paper, every project has an FDE; in practice, nobody can get time with the person who can decide.

Development of new people is harder still. A younger engineer needs to watch a senior colleague handle projects, make judgments in lower-risk situations, and review mistakes. Not every hour of that can be charged to a customer. If a bid has already removed the margin, what pays for mentoring, documentation, and postmortems?

The “staffing plan” in a bid deserves close reading. Some proposals list a senior consultant, architect, and FDE but never say how many days each will attend, whether they are shared with other projects, or who appears for a critical acceptance test. A customer reading only titles may think it bought a whole team. A supplier staring at the winning price may quietly let one person cover them all.

When an “FDE team” expands very quickly, I therefore want an accounting answer. Where did its industry experience come from? Who mentored these people? Did the projects fund enough time for them to understand customers? If the whole answer is “we put them through a standardized course,” low-cost trainees are being sold as senior experts.

## Low price is not the only problem; vague price is

Customers are right to care about cost. Expensive is not automatically good. A mature product can absorb much of the complexity, reduce on-site work, and rightly charge less. The problem is a supplier without that product capability winning on price and using an FDE story to make an oversized delivery promise.

The honest alternative is to name the scope. What does the standard product cover? Which migrations and outside interfaces are extra? What senior review is needed for high-risk workflows? If the budget is limited, start smaller and postpone automating certain actions instead of pretending that a junior person on site has four senior specialties. An honest smaller scope is better than a cheap list nobody can deliver.

Evaluation can be concrete too. Look beyond how many FDEs are listed in the staffing table to who will actually attend and for how long. Rather than accepting “industry expertise,” ask the team to walk through a difficult industry exception. Rather than accepting a demo, replay an awkward real document from the old system. A supplier with genuine people should welcome a test of judgment, not just résumé keywords.

Buyers have responsibility as well. The balance of technical and price scoring, rules for replacing key personnel during delivery, and how new scope is negotiated all shape supplier behavior. If evaluation pressures everyone to bid below the real cost and then demands a senior FDE on site throughout, the supplier is not the only party sustaining the fiction. Both sides should put scope and staffing on the table rather than share a beautiful promise nobody intends to fund.

## The vendor's own incentives bend

Sustained low bidding can distort the product roadmap. When the field discovers a general product gap, fixing it in the product consumes engineering time. A customer-specific script may get acceptance signed sooner. Under pressure, teams naturally choose the latter. More scripts make the next project more dependent on on-site development, leaving even less time to finish the product.

An FDE title is convenient at that point. It turns “the product is incomplete and we need a senior person to rescue the project” into “our advanced delivery model sends a super-individual to every customer.” The pitch sounds modern while the product problem stays in the field. If a genuine FDE writes the same kind of patch every day, the vendor is wasting its most expensive talent.

Retention suffers too. A senior person living through high-risk, underfunded, ever-expanding projects may not stay. The company may replace them with a more interchangeable junior on-site role while keeping the FDE name. The customer sees a growing “team” without seeing that the judgment available on each project has thinned.

The project retrospective needs a blunt accounting: what did the field team rescue temporarily, what became product, and what customer code still needs care? If the same product hole is patched manually by an FDE at three customers, “advanced delivery” is consuming what should be product investment. Taking recurring work back into the product is also how a vendor eventually earns room to bid honestly.

## The objection is not to a high price; it is to mismatched claims

High-value projects can carry high budgets. Some customers willingly pay for unusual delivery conditions. The concern is the common impulse to win on low price while advertising that everybody on site is an FDE, without explaining how the staffing cost is covered. I have no statistical basis to claim every project in China works this way and will not infer a whole market from one bid. But if a supplier promises low price, long-term on-site work, and a large pool of senior experts at once, it should show where the investment is.

In Oryh, we try to keep reusable record capabilities, permissions, and quantity checks in the product while Agents read company rules for what changes. If the field finds that ten payments match one bank debit, the answer should be a product that represents that fact, not another reconciliation script at every customer. Only when the product absorbs recurring problems can a limited number of senior people help deliver to many customers. Win on the lowest price and hide the shortfall behind FDE badges, and the customer and field team will eventually pay the difference.
